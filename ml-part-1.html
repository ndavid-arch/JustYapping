Teaching a Computer
to Guess Who Says Yes

Learning machine learning from zero, on real-world problems  ·  Post #2

You have 9,000 people and only 1,000 messages to send. About 1 in 9 of them will say yes to your offer. Pick at random and roughly 117 say yes. I taught a computer to pick — and it found 499.

From “My Name Is” to Machine Learning

Before this, the closest I'd come to anything “smart” in Python was a script that read a paragraph, found “my name is”, and grabbed the next two words. I wrote the rule, the computer followed it.

Machine learning flips that. You don't write the rules. You show the computer thousands of examples with the answers, and it works out the rules itself.

So I gave myself one rule: learn it on a real-world problem, not toy examples.

The Data

I used public data from a Portuguese bank that phoned 45,211 customers to offer a term deposit — you lock your money with the bank for a while, and it pays you interest. For every customer we know their age, job, balance, loans, how earlier campaigns went — and whether they said yes this time.

Only about 12% did.

This is a personal learning project on public data — not anyone's real customers.

Step 1: Counting Before Being Clever

Before any “AI”, I just counted who said yes:

It makes sense. People who trusted the bank once tend to do it again. And someone paying off a house has less spare cash to lock away. My accounting brain approved.

Step 2: Letting the Computer Learn

Then I let the computer study 36,168 customers and tested it on 9,043 it had never seen — like the difference between practising past papers and sitting the real exam. I tried three “students”:

  • The scoring sheet (logistic regression): points for each trait, added up and turned into a chance of yes.
  • The question tree (decision tree): yes/no questions asked in order, like stacking filters in Excel.
  • The forest (random forest): 300 different question trees, answers averaged — like asking 300 advisors instead of one.

The 89% Trap

My very first model was 89% accurate. I was ready to celebrate.

Then I checked a “model” that just says no to everyone. 88% accurate. When almost everybody says no, being “right” is easy — you can be right all day and still never find a single person who wants your offer.

The real question isn't “how often is it right?” It's “with the same 1,000 messages, how many interested people do we actually reach?”

The Showdown

So I gave each model the same budget: message its top 1,000 picks, then count how many really said yes.

models = {

for name, model_probs in models.items():
    top = model_probs.argsort()[::-1][:1000]   # each model's top 1,000 picks
    caught = y_test.iloc[top].sum()            # how many really said yes
    print(f"{name}: caught {caught}, wasted {1000 - caught} | hit rate {caught/1000:.0%}")
Output
Logistic regression: caught 466, wasted 534 | hit rate 47%
Decision tree: caught 377, wasted 623 | hit rate 38%
Random forest: caught 499, wasted 501 | hit rate 50%

Same 1,000 messages. The forest reached 122 more interested people than the question tree — which also means 122 fewer people annoyed by an offer they never wanted.

The Plot Twist

When I opened up the question tree to read its questions, one of them was basically: “Was this person called in March?”

In this campaign, March calls happened to go well. But that's a fact about the bank's calendar, not about the customer. Next year it means nothing. The computer will happily learn the wrong lesson — someone who understands the business still has to read its reasoning and say “mn, no.”

Turns out my accounting background isn't a detour from this. It's the part that catches the nonsense.
